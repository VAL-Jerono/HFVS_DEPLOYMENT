# Business Understanding Literature Review: Kenya’s Affordable Housing Levy, County Allocation, and Delivery Capacity

**Prepared:** 15 September 2026
**Purpose:** To establish the state of knowledge, contradictions, and defensible research gaps before formal problem formulation.

## Executive conclusion

The literature does not support treating Kenya’s Affordable Housing Programme as a simple financing problem. It is a **multi-stage public housing system** that must convert levy revenue into legally usable land, infrastructure, finance, procurement, construction, allocation, occupation, and long-term maintenance. A county may therefore receive funding and still be unable to deliver housing. Conversely, a county may have strong construction capacity while failing to reach households with the greatest housing need.

The central gap is an **allocation-accountability gap** with linked data and methods dimensions. No located peer-reviewed study evaluates whether post-2024 Affordable Housing Levy resources are allocated across Kenya’s 47 counties through a transparent, needs-responsive and capacity-adjusted rule. The official record provides legal architecture, revenue figures, project inventories, survey-based need indicators and project-level implementation evidence, but it does not yet provide a public county-by-year ledger linking:

> levy assessed and collected → Fund receipt → allocation decision → county/project commitment → cash release → land readiness → procurement → construction → service connection → beneficiary allocation → occupation → affordability and maintenance.
> 

This missing chain prevents confident answers to the questions that matter most: whether the programme is need-based, demand-based, capacity-based, politically mediated, or some combination; whether money is the binding constraint; whether construction capacity varies systematically across counties; and whether completed units improve welfare for the intended groups.

The most defensible refined problem is therefore not “how should Kenya build more affordable houses?” It is:

> **How can Kenya allocate Affordable Housing Fund resources across counties and projects using a transparent rule that separately measures housing need and affordability, delivery capacity and feasibility, equity safeguards, and downside risk, while creating an auditable link between public money and occupied, serviced, affordable housing?**
> 

The literature supports a two-dimensional architecture. **Need and affordability** should measure who requires housing support and which product they can realistically sustain. **Delivery capacity** should measure whether a county/project can convert an allocation into adequate, serviced and occupied housing within cost, time and governance constraints. These dimensions should not be collapsed into one opaque score. A high-need, low-readiness county should not be abandoned; it may require land regularisation, infrastructure, technical assistance, rental housing, upgrading or incremental finance rather than immediate unrestricted construction funds.

Kenya’s case is genuinely novel because it combines a newly statutory national levy and Fund, a constitutional county system, an incomplete public allocation record, large informal and incremental housing markets, and a current opportunity to design an evidence-based allocation and accountability system before a full decade of path dependence develops. The novelty is not simply that Kenya has an affordable-housing programme. It is the possibility of reconstructing and testing the programme’s allocation-to-outcome chain while separating welfare need from production feasibility.

## 1. What the international literature establishes

### 1.1 Housing supply is a system, not a unit target

Global housing research treats delivery as a value chain. Supply depends on land, tenure, planning rights, infrastructure, construction inputs, skilled labour, finance, procurement, institutions and household purchasing power. A funding increase can therefore fail to produce additional affordable units when the binding constraint is serviced land, approvals, utilities, contractor capacity or household affordability.

Research on housing-supply elasticity demonstrates the mechanism. Saiz shows that geography and developable land constrain the responsiveness of housing supply, while Hilber and Vermeulen show that restrictive planning conditions amplify the price effect of income shocks. The implication for Kenya is not that regulation is always the problem. It is that the binding constraint must be diagnosed locally. A county formula should identify whether the marginal constraint is land assembly, planning, infrastructure, finance, construction, procurement, household purchasing power or governance.1 2

The World Bank’s housing and urban-land diagnostics reach a similar conclusion through cross-country evidence. Land-market institutions, property registration, planning procedures, infrastructure and finance interact. Peripheral land can be inexpensive but economically unaffordable once transport, utilities and social infrastructure are included. A project that is cheap to build but distant from jobs may impose a high total cost on the household and may remain vacant or produce long-term welfare losses.3 4

The Sub-Saharan African literature adds a further qualification: formal construction is only one part of the housing system. Incremental self-build, informal rental and community savings frequently supply most housing because formal systems are too expensive, slow or administratively inaccessible. A programme that measures only formal units may mistake the dominant housing pathway for a problem to be eliminated rather than a delivery channel to be upgraded.5 6

### 1.2 Affordability and buildability are distinct tests

The literature provides a strong basis for separating **can households afford this housing?** from **can this project deliver this housing?** Stone’s residual-income approach argues that affordability is not adequately captured by a single housing-cost-to-income ratio. The relevant question is whether a household has enough income left after housing costs to meet essential non-housing needs. Household size, disability, transport, utilities, taxes and irregular income matter.7

Buildability is a supply-side feasibility test. It asks whether a specific proposal can be delivered on a specific site under real legal, physical, financial, infrastructural, organisational and market conditions. A buildability test should include legal site control, developable area, hazards, approvals, infrastructure, contractor capacity, material availability, procurement, financing, maintenance and the probability of completion.

These tests generate four distinct outcomes:

| Affordability | Buildability | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High | High | The project can potentially produce occupied housing for the intended group. |
| Low | High | Units may be completed but remain vacant, be taken by higher-income households, or create unsustainable debt. |
| High | Low | The proposed price or rent is attractive on paper, but land, approvals, infrastructure, finance or contractors prevent delivery. |
| Low | Low | The project should not receive unrestricted construction funding until its binding constraints are addressed. |

This distinction is central to Kenya because the Affordable Housing Act defines affordable housing through an income-related 30 percent ceiling, while the 2025 Regulations require assessment of an applicant’s ability to meet payment obligations. Ability to pay for a selected product is not equivalent to housing need. A household may be unable to purchase an apartment but still have severe overcrowding, rent burden, tenure insecurity or inadequate services.

### 1.3 Delivery capacity is multidimensional and stage-based

The global evidence does not define delivery capacity as a single standardised index. It instead identifies observable stages. A useful Kenya framework should measure:

1. **Need and targeting:** overcrowding, inadequate structure, water and sanitation deficits, tenure insecurity, rent burden, income and vulnerability.
2. **Land readiness:** ownership or recognised tenure, title or allotment status, disputes, encumbrances, developable area and assembly time.
3. **Planning and approvals:** steps, time, cost, revision rates, coordination failures and environmental or social safeguards.
4. **Infrastructure readiness:** roads, water, sewerage, drainage, electricity, transport and operation-and-maintenance responsibility.
5. **Project feasibility:** land, infrastructure, construction, finance, tax, professional, contingency and maintenance costs against achievable prices or rents.
6. **Construction ecosystem:** licensed contractors, skills, materials, equipment, testing, supervision, defects and productivity.
7. **Public implementation:** procurement, payments, contract variations, audit findings, intergovernmental coordination and grievance resolution.
8. **Outcome:** completed, serviced, occupied and affordable units; cost and time variance; beneficiary composition; durability and maintenance.

This framework prevents “land allocated,” “contract awarded,” “construction ongoing,” “unit completed,” and “housing delivered” from being treated as equivalent states.3 4 8

### 1.4 Earmarked levies do not automatically produce equitable housing

International experience with payroll deductions, employer contributions and compulsory housing funds is mixed. The common lesson is that a stable revenue stream is only one condition of delivery.

Brazil’s FGTS and BNH system generated large volumes of finance, but World Bank archival evidence reports that in BNH’s first decade more than one million dwelling units were financed while only about one quarter were low-income subsidised dwellings. The system therefore demonstrates a conversion gap: high financial volume does not imply proportional service to the lowest-income group.9 Mosciaro and Aalbers further show how compulsory savings can operate as asset-based welfare that is concentrated among formal workers, leaving informal workers structurally outside.10

Argentina’s FONAVI is particularly relevant to Kenya because decentralisation transferred implementation responsibilities to provincial governments. The evidence reviewed reports incomplete applicant registries, weak cost recovery, high administrative costs, weak enforcement and continued dominance of construction even where statutory purposes were broader. The lesson is not that decentralisation necessarily fails. It is that transfers should be tied to transparent performance, land and project readiness, beneficiary records and audit follow-up rather than simply passed through as unconditioned funds.11

Mexico’s INFONAVIT and China’s Housing Provident Fund show the importance of individual accounts, administrative data, financial governance and formal labour-market coverage. Singapore’s HDB demonstrates what can happen when a housing institution controls or coordinates land, construction, finance, allocation and estate management over a long period. It is not a directly transferable model for Kenya because its land, state capacity, urban scale and institutional history differ materially, but it illustrates the preconditions for an integrated system.12 13 14

France’s employer payroll contribution and the Philippines’ Pag-IBIG system provide additional warnings. Payroll contributions can support rental housing and finance, but revenue-to-unit conversion depends on clear planning, governance, transparency and evaluation. Mandatory contribution systems are also vulnerable to regressivity if they primarily serve formal workers while low-income informal households receive only indirect or residual benefits.15

The international cases therefore favour a **conditional, transparent and modular allocation model**. A national fund may contain separate windows for construction, infrastructure, rental housing, upgrading, incremental finance, guarantees, technical assistance and maintenance. Each window requires its own output and outcome indicators.

## 2. What Sub-Saharan and East African evidence adds

### 2.1 The dominant regional constraint is the formal delivery system

The World Bank’s Sub-Saharan Africa stocktaking identifies simultaneous constraints in land, infrastructure, finance, planning, registration, materials, skills and institutions. Formal mortgage access is limited, construction is often incremental and informal, and the cost of serviced land places conventional units beyond the reach of many households.5

Peer-reviewed evidence finds that housing finance in Africa was not yet reducing inequality at the levels observed in the underlying panel of 48 countries. Nguena, Tchana Tchana and Zeufack identify a possible threshold above which housing finance may reduce inequality, but the result is macro-level and does not establish county-level distributional effects.16 Makinde and Agyemang and Morrison show how land ownership, infrastructure costs, slow approvals, customary tenure, weak local planning and high finance costs interact in West African housing delivery.17 18

South African evidence shows why numerical output is not enough. Large-scale delivery on cheap peripheral land can reproduce spatial inequality, increase transport costs, limit access to jobs and services and create new poverty enclaves. The South African subsidy literature also reports uneven evaluation evidence, making it difficult to establish whether the programme built durable assets for the poor or merely delivered structures.19 20

For Kenya, this means that the unit target must be supplemented by location, accessibility, tenure, services, affordability after transport and occupancy. A completed unit that is unaffordable, disconnected or vacant is not equivalent to an adequate housing outcome.

### 2.2 Tanzania, Uganda, Rwanda and Ethiopia are modular comparators, not templates

**Tanzania** provides the strongest comparator for separating wholesale finance from construction. The Tanzania Housing Finance Project and Tanzania Mortgage Refinance Company expanded the mortgage market, increased participating lenders and supported housing microfinance. This is a finance-market result, not proof that the national housing shortage was solved. Tanzania’s land and planning audit found that only a small share of master plans had been approved over the audited period, with compensation, land disputes and land-information systems remaining significant constraints. Kenya can transfer the wholesale-liquidity and incremental-finance functions, but only with a separate land-readiness gate.21 22

**Uganda** shows the limits of an enabling policy when tenure and income are informal. Its national policy emphasises government enablement and partnership rather than a large national construction subsidy. Fragmented tenure, limited title coverage, informal employment and high interest rates restrict mortgage access. Kampala evidence finds that tenure arrangements influence informal housing and land-use outcomes. Kenya can learn from incremental and enabling approaches, but a mortgage-led model would exclude many households without flexible underwriting, rental products, upgrading and tenure-sensitive finance.23 24

**Rwanda** demonstrates that finance can outperform serviced affordable-unit delivery. The housing-finance project exceeded loan targets, while infrastructure-supported affordable units lagged substantially. Kigali research finds that scheme units can remain unaffordable for much of the target population. Rwanda therefore supports separating credit outputs, land and infrastructure outputs, completed units and affordability outcomes. Its centralised governance is not directly transferable to Kenya’s constitutional county system.25

**Ethiopia** demonstrates the risks of scale-first condominium delivery. Large public programmes produced many units, but lower-income households often could not meet down-payment and mortgage requirements. Gondar evidence also identifies weak post-construction administration. Ethiopia is useful as a warning against treating unit counts as success without affordability, land-use efficiency, occupation, maintenance and beneficiary tracking.26 27

The East African comparison supports a **two-ledger model**. The first ledger should report revenue, financial intermediation, loans, guarantees, subsidies and repayment. The second should report land, infrastructure, procurement, construction, service connections, occupancy and maintenance. A financial success in the first ledger must not be presented as a physical delivery success in the second.

## 3. Kenya’s legal and institutional business understanding

### 3.1 What the Affordable Housing Act fixes

The Affordable Housing Act, 2024 creates a 1.5 percent levy on employee gross salary and on gross income not otherwise covered by the employment rule. The employer remits an equal employer contribution. KRA is the collector, and payment is due by the ninth working day after the relevant month. The Act creates the Affordable Housing Fund and Board and permits Fund resources to support housing, infrastructure, long-term finance, maintenance and related services.28 29

The Act establishes a County Rural and Urban Affordable Housing Committee in every county. County committees must prepare five-year and annual investment programmes, coordinate infrastructure planning, report to the Board and report annually to county assemblies. The First Schedule lists potential implementing agencies, including county governments, the National Housing Corporation, the National Construction Authority, the State Department responsible for housing and approved private-sector agencies.

The Act also fixes public-participation and land safeguards. County-held land cannot be allocated without participation and stakeholder engagement. Projects affecting existing settlements require notice, a resettlement mechanism and a first-right-of-purchase framework for residents. The Act requires Fund accounts to be submitted for audit and creates penalties for misappropriation and false information.

### 3.2 What remains discretionary or unobserved

The Act does **not** publish a county-by-county formula for the main pool of project capital. Section 11 permits allocation to county governments under the Public Finance Management Act and conditions prescribed by the National Treasury. The Act requires at least 0.5 percent of Fund monies for county committees as a conditional grant, but that is a committee-administration floor, not a formula giving counties 0.5 percent of the Fund for construction. It should not be misreported as an automatic county share.

The Board retains discretion over project selection, implementing agencies, timing, unit mix, off-take, financing agreements and investment programmes. The 2025 Regulations make household administration more specific. They provide for citizenship, age, previous-benefit restrictions, electronic applications, a deposit of up to 5 percent, verification, payment-capacity assessment, income pools, deposit assistance, default procedures and appeals. They do not establish a transparent county allocation formula, a public poverty score or an auditable geographic ranking procedure.30

The legal history is also important. The 2023 levy litigation concerned the earlier Finance Act mechanism and should not be cited as if it directly invalidated the separate 2024 Affordable Housing Act. The 2024 High Court decision upheld the later Act’s facial constitutionality but did not create a county distribution formula or resolve future disputes over procurement, land, conditional grants or implementation.31

### 3.3 Institutional responsibilities and overlaps

The system is distributed across institutions:

| Institution | Primary role | Evidence gap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| KRA | Levy assessment, collection and remittance | Common-period reconciliation between collection, remittance, commission and Fund receipt. |
| Affordable Housing Board | Fund management, allocation, oversight, monitoring and investment programmes | Public project-level allocation register, decision criteria and versioned investment programmes. |
| National Treasury | Appropriation, conditions, transfers, fiscal control and investment approval | County conditional-grant releases, conditions and timing. |
| County governments and committees | Land, planning, infrastructure, county programmes and reporting | Committee constitution, minutes, land records, plans and county receipts. |
| NCA | Construction regulation and sector capacity | Link between contractor performance, audited costs, project outcomes and county allocation. |
| Auditor-General and Controller of Budget | Independent audit, expenditure and public-finance oversight | Finding-to-action closure and a housing-specific county series. |
| Developers, contractors, utilities and financiers | Design, finance, construction, connection and handover | Contract, variation, payment, quality, service and occupancy linkage. |

The mandates form a chain, but the public evidence does not yet show whether information moves across that chain in a common identifier and time base.

## 4. Kenya’s revenue, project and audit evidence

### 4.1 Revenue is substantial but not equivalent to housing output

The Auditor-General’s special audit reports KSh 114.522 billion collected by KRA between June 2023 and April 2025, KSh 111.316 billion remitted to programme accounts, KSh 2.383 billion in cash in transit and KSh 0.823 billion retained as a collection commission under a pre-Act service-level arrangement. It reports KSh 34.721 billion credited to the pre-Act Housing Fund Tax Account and KSh 76.595 billion transferred to the post-Act Affordable Housing Fund Account.32

For the pre-Act account, the audit reports KSh 14.510 billion applied to implementation, KSh 20.000 billion invested in Treasury bills, KSh 0.195 billion refunded to the National Treasury and a small residual balance at the audit cut-off. These figures distinguish **collected**, **remitted**, **invested**, **applied to projects** and **spent on completed units**. They should never be collapsed into one “housing delivered” figure.

AHB workbooks report larger cumulative figures across pre-Act and post-Act sections, but they mix collection periods, labels, “Finance Act” and Affordable Housing Act sections, and formula-derived totals. The April 2026 workbook includes a 31 December 2026 row despite its stated April cut-off. These workbooks are useful administrative leads, not independently reconciled accounts. A final study should retain each workbook version and reconcile it to bank statements and audited Fund accounts.

### 4.2 The audited physical portfolio was early and uneven

The special audit records 842 projects presented across all 47 counties. The portfolio includes affordable housing, institutional housing, social housing, markets, roads, drainage, floodlights and other infrastructure. In the 142-project Affordable Housing Project category, physical verification found zero completed, 60 ongoing, 78 not started and four not independently verified at the audit cut-off. Ongoing projects ranged from 1 to 98 percent completion. The audit itself contains portfolio-count inconsistencies, including totals of 841, 842 and 854 in different passages. Those inconsistencies must be preserved in the research database rather than silently corrected.

This evidence supports a large, nationally distributed portfolio and substantial implementation exposure. It does not establish that every county received a defined amount of levy capital. The presence of projects across all 47 counties is not the same as a county allocation ledger, and a project inventory is not a beneficiary register.

The NCA’s 2026 study provides an important but bounded productivity signal. Its DEA of 58 AHP projects found no project on the relative efficiency frontier, a mean score of about 0.33 and approximately 74 percent of projects below 0.50. The study identifies examples from Nairobi, Kwale, Laikipia, Mombasa, Nyeri and Meru. However, DEA scores are relative to the sample and depend on selected inputs, outputs, data quality and environmental conditions. The study is not peer-reviewed, does not link levy amounts to projects, does not provide county denominators and cannot support a county-government efficiency ranking.33

### 4.3 What the audits imply

The audit evidence points to feasibility, land, procurement, utilities, supervision, contract management and transition risks. It also shows why a clean financial-statement opinion does not eliminate project-level risk. The Fund’s property, plant and equipment work-in-progress can be accounted for while land ownership, service connection, completion and occupancy remain unresolved.

The correct business-understanding conclusion is therefore:

> **Kenya has demonstrated revenue mobilisation and programme activity, but the publicly available evidence does not yet demonstrate county-level allocation equity, cost per occupied unit, beneficiary incidence, affordability after occupation, or value for money.**
> 

## 5. Kenya’s housing-need evidence and its limits

### 5.1 The 2023/24 Kenya Housing Survey is a strong baseline

The 2023/24 Kenya Housing Survey covers all 47 counties and used 25,900 sampled households across 1,295 clusters, with 21,347 completed interviews and a reported household response rate of 92.1 percent. It measures tenure, crowding, construction materials, water, sanitation, energy, rent, land security, disability access, employment and income. It is the strongest current source for descriptive county need comparisons, provided weights and precision are used.34

The survey reports 78.8 percent of dwelling units as inadequate on its consolidated housing-quality index. It reports 44.1 percent passing the structural-fitness measure, 59.7 percent passing the combined water-and-sanitation measure and 31.9 percent unfit on crowding. Rural structural and service deprivation is substantially higher, while urban areas show much greater rental dependence and affordability pressure.

The KHS also shows why rental dependence must not be translated automatically into mortgage demand. Urban home ownership is low and rental dependence is high, but many tenants may require rental housing, upgrading, incremental construction, better services or tenure protection rather than immediate ownership products. A national ownership target can therefore be misaligned with actual household demand.

### 5.2 The survey is not a beneficiary or impact database

The KHS is cross-sectional. It cannot identify every informal-settlement resident, verify eligibility, track applicants, measure causal effects of the AHP or establish whether a project is additional to housing that would otherwise have been built. It should be joined to the 2019 census for scale, the 2022 poverty report for poverty depth and vulnerable groups, labour data for income volatility, geospatial data for settlement and service deficits, and AHP administrative records for applications, savings, allocations, projects and occupation.

The KHS and census contain different deficit measures. The census reports an approximate dwelling-unit deficit of 8.89 million, while KHS material refers to a habitable-room deficit of 14.7 million. These are not contradictory estimates of the same quantity. They use different units, definitions and reference periods. Any allocation model must state whether it measures a stock deficit, flow deficit, adequacy deficit, affordability deficit or vulnerability deficit.

The survey also contains documented internal inconsistencies in the exposed report, including a conflict over clean and unclean energy wording and differing tenure graphics and prose. These are not reasons to reject the KHS. They are reasons to build a reproducible data dictionary, retain the source table and document corrections.

### 5.3 Need, vulnerability and readiness must remain separate

A defensible allocation model should have at least three pillars:

- **Need and scale:** overcrowding, inadequate structure, services, population, household formation, rent burden and tenure.
- **Vulnerability:** poverty depth, disability, gender, age, household composition, informal tenure, eviction or demolition exposure and marginalisation.
- **Readiness and safeguards:** land, plans, utilities, procurement, county capacity, public participation and legal compliance.

Readiness should not erase need. If readiness is used as a pure reward, the formula will permanently favour counties that already have land, staff and administrative advantages. The appropriate use of readiness is to determine the **type and sequence of support**: construction capital where sites are ready; land and planning assistance where they are not; rental, upgrading or incremental finance where ownership products are unsuitable.

## 6. Nairobi as a validation case

Nairobi is the **best stress test and the worst standalone generalisation case**. It concentrates high land and infrastructure costs, a large informal-rental market, insecure and hybrid tenure, dense construction, high demand, multiple national–county interfaces and substantial AHP activity. It is therefore ideal for testing whether an allocation model recognises local constraints rather than relying on national averages.

Nairobi’s informal settlements exhibit a low-quality, high-price rental market. Gulyani and Talukdar’s household study found that higher-quality units command higher rents but that rental income does not automatically translate into adequate living conditions. Comparative Nairobi–Dakar evidence finds that tenants can be worse off than owners despite paying substantial rent. Huchzermeyer documents high-density private landlordism and multi-storey tenements, showing real supply response alongside regulatory, safety and infrastructure stress.35 36 37

Nairobi’s tenure systems are hybrid. Pseudo-customary and pseudo-formal practices coexist with official documents, local recognition, written agreements and community governance. A binary titled/untitled variable will therefore miss the institutions that affect both displacement risk and infrastructure feasibility. Participatory enumeration can also exclude tenants and marginalised residents if landlords or local elites dominate representation.38 39

The World Bank’s Nairobi-relevant evidence reports high land costs, scarce serviced land, approval delays, jurisdictional uncertainty and a substantial land share of urban housing costs. These are historical baseline figures, not current price estimates, but the mechanism remains material. A national affordability scenario based on a KSh 1.7 million house and a 15-year mortgage cannot be interpreted as observed Nairobi affordability. It may use the wrong price, product, income documentation and tenure assumption for informal renters.3 40

Nairobi also complicates county attribution because its labour and housing markets extend into Kiambu, Kajiado and Machakos. A project outside Nairobi may serve Nairobi workers, while a Nairobi project may create transport or displacement effects in neighbouring counties. The model should therefore include metropolitan externalities and within-county settlement variation.

Nairobi should be used as one validation stratum, not as the national calibration sample. The minimum comparison should include Nairobi, a secondary city and a rural or arid county, using common definitions but locally varying costs and capacities.

## 7. Methods literature and a defensible analytical architecture

### 7.1 Targeting and need estimation

Proxy means tests can assist targeting where full means testing is infeasible, but evidence across African countries shows that standard PMTs can exclude many poor households. Poverty-quantile models, out-of-sample validation and transparent error reporting are preferable to in-sample fit alone.41 42

Machine learning can complement administrative targeting but cannot silently replace legal eligibility, human review or appeal. A Togo study using mobile-phone data improved some exclusion outcomes relative to geographic targeting but performed worse than a hypothetical comprehensive social registry and raised privacy, fairness and temporal-degradation concerns.43 Any Kenyan model should test coverage and error by county, gender, disability, rural/urban residence, informal-sector status and housing category.

The model should be housing-specific. Consumption poverty alone is insufficient. Need indicators should include overcrowding, structure, water, sanitation, energy, tenure security, rent burden, eviction exposure and accessibility. A household unable to afford ownership may still be eligible for rental housing, upgrading or service support.

### 7.2 Efficiency and delivery capacity

DEA, stochastic frontier analysis and related efficiency methods can benchmark relative performance, but they do not measure social justice or need. Smith and Street warn that weights, production functions, environmental conditions and dynamic effects are policy choices. Kapsoli, Mogues and Verdier show that public-investment efficiency scores can be useful but remain sensitive to proxy outputs and aggregation.44 45

For Kenya, capacity analysis should be conditional on land, infrastructure, approvals, procurement and environmental constraints. A county should not be labelled inefficient because it produces fewer units when its sites are legally unresolved or unserviced. An efficiency model should report uncertainty, environmental controls, time dynamics and sensitivity to inputs and outputs.

The NCA DEA study can be treated as a starting hypothesis about project-level variation. It cannot be used as the dependent variable for a county allocation formula until project inputs, audited expenditure, completion, quality, service connection and occupancy are linked.

### 7.3 Allocation under uncertainty

Levy receipts, construction costs, interest rates, land clearance, contractor completion, applicant demand, default and occupancy are uncertain. A two-stage model is appropriate: first allocate an envelope or readiness support; then permit recourse through phasing, reallocation or unit-mix changes when information arrives. Stochastic programming is appropriate when probability distributions are defensible. Robust or distributionally robust optimisation is preferable when only bounds or moments can be defended.46 47 48

The allocation objective should not be “maximise units.” It should report expected and worst-case outcomes:

- households receiving adequate support;
- target-group coverage;
- completed, serviced and occupied units;
- affordability after utilities and transport;
- unspent or stranded funds;
- delay and cost risk;
- geographic and vulnerable-group equity;
- maintenance and repayment sustainability.

A lexicographic architecture is preferable where statutory, safety and equity floors are non-negotiable. First satisfy legal and minimum-equity constraints, then optimise social coverage and affordability, then compare efficiency. If a weighted model is used, publish weights, normalisation, missing-data treatment and rank stability.

### 7.4 Suggested empirical sequence

The literature supports a sequential mixed-method design:

1. **Reconstruct the rules.** Code the Act, Regulations, Boma Yangu instructions, investment programmes, Board guidance, county plans and observed administrative practice.
2. **Build the county/project panel.** Link collections, Fund receipts, allocations, releases, contracts, land, approvals, construction, service connections, beneficiaries, occupation and maintenance.
3. **Estimate need and affordability separately.** Use KHS, census, poverty and administrative data. Validate inclusion and exclusion errors against housing deprivation and actual take-up.
4. **Estimate capacity separately.** Use readiness indicators and a conditional efficiency or completion model. Preserve environmental constraints and uncertainty.
5. **Model allocation scenarios.** Compare population, need-only, readiness-only, need-plus-readiness, staged and robust alternatives.
6. **Audit fairness and error.** Test results by county, rural/urban setting, income, tenure, gender, disability, informal-sector status and housing product.
7. **Specify decision use.** Identify which institution would act on each output and what corrective action follows from a low score.

## 8. Complete gap map

### 8.1 Data gaps

The decisive data gap is the absence of a public, versioned county/project panel. Required fields include levy assessment and collection, Fund receipt, investment and administration deductions, Board allocation decision, Treasury conditions, county receipt, project commitment, contract and variation, payment certificate, land evidence, approvals, utilities, physical progress, completed units, allocated units, occupied units, beneficiary characteristics, payment terms, arrears, maintenance and audit findings.

Other gaps include:

- no public county-by-year levy allocation formula or disbursement register;
- no reconciled applicant, saver, beneficiary and occupancy data;
- no common definitions of planned, ongoing, complete, serviced, allocated and occupied;
- no complete project-level cost and quality panel;
- weak linkage between KHS need indicators and programme decisions;
- incomplete land-title and site-readiness evidence;
- no public time series for Boma Yangu counts and decision rules;
- no systematic record of appeals, exclusions and grievances.

### 8.2 Methods gaps

The literature provides targeting, efficiency and optimisation methods, but not a validated integrated model for county housing allocation in Kenya. The specific methods gap is how to combine need, affordability, buildability, capacity and uncertainty without rewarding already advantaged counties or hiding normative choices.

A further gap is causal evaluation. The available evidence does not establish whether the levy increases net housing supply, changes land prices, crowds out private or informal finance, improves welfare relative to rental or upgrading alternatives, or transfers benefits disproportionately to formal workers.

### 8.3 Institutional-accountability gaps

The formal accountability chain is stronger than the public evidence of operational accountability. The Act requires plans, reports and audits, but the research record does not yet show whether those documents are timely, comparable, public, reconciled and acted upon. The key gap is not the absence of institutions; it is the absence of **traceable institutional handoffs** and a finding-to-corrective-action system.

### 8.4 Equity and external-validity gaps

Formal-worker-funded systems risk regressivity. Demand-based allocation can favour better-connected counties and households with documented income. Readiness-based allocation can favour counties that already possess land and technical staff. Nairobi-specific models can misfit rural and arid counties. National averages can hide tenant, disability, gender, informal-work and settlement-level exclusion.

The model must therefore distinguish equality of allocation, equity of opportunity, equality of output, and equality of outcome. It should publish subgroup coverage and error rather than only county ranks.

## 9. Refined problem statement and research questions

### Refined problem statement

Kenya’s Affordable Housing Act establishes a national levy and Fund with county participation, but the public evidence does not yet reveal a transparent, validated or auditable rule for allocating project resources across counties. Existing evidence measures housing need, levy collection, project presence and selected aspects of construction performance separately. It does not connect those measures into a common county/project allocation-to-occupancy chain. Consequently, policymakers cannot determine whether observed shortfalls arise from inadequate revenue, poor allocation, land and infrastructure constraints, weak implementation capacity, unsuitable affordability assumptions, targeting error or institutional accountability failure.

### Primary research question

**How can Kenya design and validate a transparent, needs-responsive and delivery-capacity-adjusted allocation framework for Affordable Housing Fund resources that improves the probability of producing occupied, serviced and affordable housing across counties?**

### Sub-questions

1. What allocation rule is legally authorised, administratively used and empirically observable under the Act, Regulations, investment programmes and portal instructions?
2. How should housing need, vulnerability and affordability be measured for formal and informal households using KHS, census, poverty and administrative data?
3. Which land, infrastructure, procurement, construction, governance and finance indicators best measure county/project delivery capacity?
4. How large are the gaps between levy collection, Fund receipt, allocation, release, expenditure, completion, occupation and affordability?
5. Does a need-only, readiness-only, population-based, staged or robust allocation model produce materially different county outcomes?
6. What are the inclusion, exclusion, spatial and subgroup errors under each model?
7. What data-governance, audit, appeal and public-disclosure mechanisms are required for legitimate administrative use?

## 10. What “done” should mean for policymakers

The research will be useful outside academia if it produces:

- a versioned county/project allocation register reconciled to Fund accounts;
- a transparent needs and vulnerability dashboard with definitions and uncertainty;
- a separate delivery-readiness and capacity dashboard;
- project-level status definitions and evidence requirements;
- an affordability test that includes utilities, transport and residual income;
- scenario comparisons showing who gains and loses under alternative rules;
- a staged funding and recourse protocol;
- an audit trail from levy collection to occupation;
- a subgroup fairness and error report;
- a correction protocol identifying which institution acts when a bottleneck is detected.

The cost of a wrong model is not merely statistical. At county level it may lock money into unbuildable sites, reward already-capable areas, leave high-need households without support or create completed but unaffordable and unoccupied assets. At household level it may produce unaffordable debt, displacement, loss of livelihood, exclusion from a benefit or a false claim that housing need has been solved.
